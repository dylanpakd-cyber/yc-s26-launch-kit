# YC Waitlist Email: core knowledge

## What waitlist people are
They are warm, not committed. They signed up for a reason. The job is to remind them of that reason, remove the one doubt that holds them back, and give them a real reason to act on a real date. Do not sell harder. Make it easier to say yes to something they already half wanted.

The list is the cheapest source of first customers. So once the window opens, stop warming up. Convert the people who are ready, answer the people who are not, and run a clean close.

## Persuasion map (one email, one lever)
| Lever | Sounds like | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| Remind the reason | "You asked to hear when this was ready. It is." | Launch email |
| Reciprocity | Give one useful thing before the ask | Warm-up, nurture |
| Name the doubt | The most common reason people hesitate, then the honest answer | Middle |
| Show it working | A demo, a real customer in their own words (with permission), a plain before and after | Middle |
| Liking and unity | The founder note, "people like us", the shared problem | Middle, never launch day |
| Commitment | A reply ask: "Hit reply and tell me the one thing you would use it for" | Warm-up, welcome |
| Make the offer clear | What they get, what it costs, what the guarantee really is | Every selling email, one block |
| Real deadline | The date the offer truly changes | Last days only |
| Honest admission | One true, slightly costly limit, then the one thing it does best | Once, where it makes the main claim land harder |

Social proof only with permission and a source. Scarcity only if real.

## Subject lines
- Write five, pick one, show the pick. Keep it to about 40 to 45 characters so the hook shows on a phone. The email check fails anything over 45.
- Casual and human. One idea. No price in the subject.
- The preview text finishes the thought and never repeats the subject.
- Banned: all caps, fake "Re:" or "Fwd:", more than one "!", emoji chains, curiosity the email does not pay off.
- Guess test: if a reader can guess the whole email from the subject, the line is weak unless the email is plain logistics (launch, last call).
- Patterns and worked examples: yc-waitlist-email-references ("Subject line bank").

## The five deadline tests
All five checks below must pass before a time limit appears in a draft.
1. Confirm the exact cutoff is set in the checkout. After it, a buyer cannot get the old offer.
2. Give the reader an unambiguous calendar point: date, clock time, and named zone.
3. Explain the practical change at that point, such as a different plan or closed intake.
4. Compare all drafts against one master cutoff. Every mention must resolve to that same instant.
5. Do not extend or restart the window. A later opening needs a separate, genuine reason, disclosed to readers.
Name the change rather than count down. If there is no enforceable cutoff, write about the problem the reader still has or the outcome they want. Do not add a clock.
## Honest scarcity
Allowed when true at send time: a real end date, a real limit from production or capacity, a real price change. Not allowed: reset timers, counts you cannot verify, "only a few left" without a number read from the source system that day. If a count is used, log where it came from and when.

## Objection matrix (filled from the owner's own data)
For each top objection: the buyer's phrase, the honest answer in one or two sentences, the proof, the email that answers it, and what the answer does NOT claim. Common families: price, trust in a new brand, "will it work for me", timing, risk if it fails, shipping or access, switching from what they use now. Rank by how many real readers raised it. Never invent one. If the owner has no data, the plan says "objections assumed, confirm with replies" and the first warm-up asks readers what is stopping them.

## Email types and shapes
- **Announcement (day before):** tomorrow it opens, what it is. Direct. No story wrapper.
- **Launch:** it is ready; one paragraph of what it is; the offer block; one link.
- **Resend:** same job, new subject, new first line, to non-openers only.
- **Story:** a short true scene, the point, the action.
- **Answer:** the question, the answer, the proof, the action.
- **Demo or proof:** show it working; one image or clip; one action.
- **Founder note:** why this exists and one honest limit. Midway, never launch day.
- **Deadline day:** the real date in the first line and what changes after it.
- **Last hours:** under 120 words; deadline first; one link; nothing new but fresh proof.
- **Close:** thank buyers; tell non-buyers what happens next, only if true. No guilt.
- **Thank-you or bridge:** acknowledges the action and says what happens next.
Every email has one job, one reader segment, one primary link, at most one deadline, one subject chosen from five.

## The stable offer block
Each selling email has a lead that changes (a question, a proof, a story) and an offer block that does not: what you get, the terms, the price rule, the deadline line, the link. Copy the block from the brief. Only the deadline line changes ("Thursday", then "tomorrow", then "tonight"). If the price or terms change, that is a new window.

## Cadence defaults
See yc-waitlist-email-references ("Send timing defaults") for the day map. In short: announcement the day before, offer on day one, one email a day through the middle, a morning and a last-hours email on the final day, a close note after. Cap selling emails to people who never engage. Stop after the last-hours email.

## Voice facts
Voice is rules, not adjectives: average sentence length, how often a fragment appears, contractions yes or no, how the reader is addressed, the sign-off, words always used, words never used. A founder with no past emails starts from the default starter voice in yc-waitlist-email-references ("Brief and voice template") and confirms it after the first three emails.

## Plain-writing moves (used in the edit pass)
- Name the exact reader and the one question this email answers before writing.
- Every sentence must reveal something new. Cut the ones that do not.
- Put the strongest word near the end of a sentence. Two commas at most.
- Short first line, a short block that explains, one short line that lands.
- Aim for clarity a nine-year-old could follow, without baby talk.
- End on the last concrete sentence, not a polished summary line.

## Never rules
Never invent a product, claim, price, quantity or date. Never promise what the page does not. Never write fake urgency. Never mail buyers a "buy now" message. Never send or schedule. Never imitate a real person's writing beyond the owner's own approved samples. Never copy another brand's emails or a paid course's lesson text.

Revision cap: two revision rounds per piece of work. After round 2, stop, show the best version with its open flags, and ask the owner how to go on.
